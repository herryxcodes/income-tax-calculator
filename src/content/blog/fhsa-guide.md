The First Home Savings Account gives you a tax deduction on contributions and tax-free withdrawals when you buy a home. No other registered account in Canada does both. If you're a first-time home buyer, this should be the first account you open.

## How the FHSA Works

The FHSA launched on April 1, 2023. For 2026 you can contribute up to **$8,000** a year, with a **$40,000 lifetime limit**. Contributions are tax-deductible, meaning they reduce your taxable income the same way RRSP contributions do. When you withdraw the money for a qualifying home purchase, you pay zero tax on the withdrawal — growth included.

Compare that to your other options. An RRSP gives you the deduction but taxes withdrawals. A TFSA gives you tax-free withdrawals but no deduction. The FHSA gives you both.

## Who Qualifies

You need to meet all of these when you open the account:

- **Canadian resident**
- **Age 18 or older** (in provinces where the age of majority is 19, you may need to wait until 19), and no older than 71 at the end of the year you open it
- **First-time home buyer**: you didn't live, as your principal residence, in a home owned by you or your current spouse or common-law partner at any time this calendar year or in the four preceding calendar years

That last point is the one that trips people up. If your current spouse owned a home that you lived in three years ago, you don't qualify, even if your name was never on the title. The four-year lookback covers your spouse's home as well as your own.

## Contribution Room and Carry Forward

The annual limit is $8,000 and the lifetime cap is $40,000. If you don't contribute the full $8,000 in a given year, you can carry forward up to $8,000 of unused room to the following year.

This means the maximum you can contribute in a single year is **$16,000** — your current year's $8,000 plus $8,000 carried forward from the previous year. You can't carry forward more than $8,000 regardless of how many years you've missed.

A practical example: you opened an FHSA in 2025 and contributed $3,000. In 2026, your available room is $8,000 (current year) plus $5,000 (carried forward from 2025) = $13,000. If you contribute nothing in 2026, you carry forward $8,000 (the maximum) into 2027.

## The Tax Benefit in Dollars

Using 2026 Ontario rates for employment income: if you earn $75,000, an $8,000 FHSA contribution saves you **$2,522** in tax. That's a little more than your 29.65% marginal rate would suggest, because the deduction also drops you below an Ontario Health Premium threshold. Do this every year for five years and you'll have $40,000 in the account with about **$12,610** in cumulative tax savings (assuming the same income and 2026 rates each year). That's before any investment growth.

At $120,000, the same $8,000 saves you $3,011 a year — about $15,056 over five years. (That's less than the 43.41% marginal rate at that income, because part of the deduction falls into a lower bracket.)

You don't have to claim the deduction in the year you contribute. Unused FHSA deductions can be carried forward, so if you expect a raise, you can contribute now and deduct later when your rate is higher.

## What You Can Hold Inside the Account

An FHSA can hold the same types of investments as an RRSP or TFSA:

- GICs and savings deposits
- Stocks and ETFs
- Bonds
- Mutual funds

Most major banks and brokerages offer FHSA accounts. If you're saving for a home purchase that's three to five years away, a diversified portfolio of low-cost ETFs or a high-interest savings account are the two most common approaches.

## Making a Qualifying Withdrawal

To withdraw from your FHSA tax-free, you must meet all of these at the time of the withdrawal:

- You're still a first-time home buyer
- You have a written agreement to buy or build a qualifying home located in Canada, with the purchase or construction completed before October 1 of the year after the withdrawal
- You intend to live in the home as your principal residence within one year of buying or building it
- You haven't owned the home for more than 30 days before the withdrawal
- You're a resident of Canada from the withdrawal until you buy the home

You request the withdrawal from your financial institution using Form RC725. A qualifying withdrawal isn't included in your income.

Unlike the Home Buyers' Plan from your RRSP, there is **no repayment requirement** for FHSA withdrawals. The money is yours, free and clear.

## Combining the FHSA with the Home Buyers' Plan

This is where things get interesting. You're allowed to use both the FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan for the same home purchase, as long as you meet the conditions for each withdrawal.

- **FHSA:** withdraw your balance — up to $40,000 of contributions plus any growth — with no repayment
- **HBP from RRSP:** withdraw up to $60,000 — must repay over 15 years
- **Combined total:** **$100,000** in tax-advantaged funds for your first home, plus any FHSA growth

For a couple where both partners qualify, that number doubles. Two FHSAs ($80,000) plus two HBPs ($120,000) = $200,000 from registered accounts. Even in an expensive housing market, that's a meaningful down payment.

## What Happens If You Don't Buy a Home

Life changes. Maybe you decide not to buy, or maybe you can't find the right place. The FHSA has a built-in safety net: you can **transfer the balance to your RRSP** without using any of your RRSP contribution room.

This is a genuinely good deal. You already claimed the tax deduction when you contributed to the FHSA. Now you're moving the money to your RRSP — where it continues to grow tax-deferred — without eating into your RRSP room. It's like getting free RRSP space.

The account has a lifespan, though. Your FHSA must be closed by December 31 of the earliest of these years:

- The year of the **15th anniversary** of opening your first FHSA
- The year you turn **71**
- The year after your **first qualifying withdrawal**

If you haven't used the money or transferred it to an RRSP or RRIF by then, whatever is left is added to your taxable income for that year.

## FHSA vs RRSP Home Buyers' Plan: Which Is Better?

Both help you save for a home purchase using pre-tax dollars. But the FHSA wins on one big point: withdrawals don't need to be repaid.

With the HBP, you borrow from your own RRSP and must repay the full amount over 15 years. Miss a repayment and the CRA adds that year's required amount to your taxable income. It's not a loan from the government — it's a loan from your future self, and it has consequences if you fall behind.

With the FHSA, you contribute, you claim the deduction, you withdraw for a home purchase, and you're done. No strings.

That said, there's no reason to choose one over the other. Use both. Max your FHSA first ($8,000/year is manageable for many savers), build up your [RRSP](/rrsp-calculator) alongside it, and deploy both accounts when you're ready to buy.

## Opening an FHSA: Timing Matters

Even if you can only contribute a small amount right now, open the account. Your room only starts accumulating once the account exists. Open an FHSA in 2026 and contribute nothing, and you'll have $16,000 of room in 2027. Wait until 2027 to open it, and you'll have $8,000.

Opening the account is what starts the clock -- you don't need to contribute in the first year. Just remember the deadline is different from the RRSP's: FHSA contributions made in January or February 2027 can't be deducted on your 2026 return, so anything you want to count for 2026 has to go in by December 31, 2026.
