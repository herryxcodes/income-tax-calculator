The 2026 Canadian federal tax brackets are: 14% on income up to $58,523, 20.5% on $58,523 to $117,045, 26% on $117,045 to $181,440, 29% on $181,440 to $258,482, and 33% on income over $258,482. The federal basic personal amount is $16,452, so you pay no federal tax on the first $16,452 of income. Provincial tax is added on top at rates that vary by province.

2026 is the first full year at the 14% lowest rate. The rate was cut from 15% to 14% on July 1, 2025, so 2025 returns used a blended 14.5%. The bracket thresholds also rose 2% for inflation. Below are the 2026 federal and provincial brackets, how progressive tax works, and what it means for your take-home pay. These are the rates you'll use on the 2026 return you file in spring 2027.

## 2026 Federal Tax Brackets

Canada has five federal income tax brackets. They apply to your taxable income, which is what's left after deductions such as [RRSP contributions](/rrsp-calculator), union dues and other eligible expenses are subtracted from your total income.

| Taxable Income Range | 2026 Federal Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to $58,523 | 14% |
| $58,523 to $117,045 | 20.5% |
| $117,045 to $181,440 | 26% |
| $181,440 to $258,482 | 29% |
| Over $258,482 | 33% |

The federal basic personal amount for 2026 is **$16,452**, so the first $16,452 of your income is effectively tax-free at the federal level. It's applied as a non-refundable tax credit worth $16,452 × 14% = $2,303. If your net income is above $181,440, the amount shrinks gradually, down to $14,829 at $258,482 and above.

## What Changed From 2025

In 2025 the lowest federal bracket was taxed at a blended 14.5%: 15% for January to June and 14% for July to December, after Bill C-4 cut the rate partway through the year. For 2026, 14% applies to the whole year.

Non-refundable credits such as the basic personal amount are also calculated at 14%, which takes back part of the saving. For an employee, the full-year 14% rate means about $285 less federal tax at $50,000 than the old 15% rate would have charged, and about $360 less for anyone earning more than $58,523. That's roughly double the 2025 saving.

If your non-refundable credit claims are unusually large (for example, big medical or tuition amounts), a temporary top-up tax credit keeps a 15% rate on the part of those claims above $58,523, so the rate cut can't leave you worse off. See [the tax changes for 2026](/blog/tax-changes-canada) for details.

## How Progressive Tax Actually Works

One of the most common misconceptions about Canadian taxes is the belief that moving into a higher bracket means all of your income gets taxed at the higher rate. This is not how it works.

Canada uses a **progressive (or marginal) tax system**. Each bracket rate only applies to the income that falls within that specific range. Here is a concrete example.

Suppose you have $90,000 of taxable income in 2026. Your federal tax would be calculated as follows:

1. **First $16,452**: no tax (covered by the basic personal amount credit)
2. **$16,452 to $58,523**: taxed at 14% = $5,890
3. **$58,523 to $90,000**: taxed at 20.5% = $6,453

Your total federal tax would be approximately **$12,343** before other credits (such as CPP, EI and the Canada employment amount), an effective (average) federal rate of about 13.7%. That's well below the 20.5% marginal rate you technically fall into.

The takeaway: earning more money and landing in a higher bracket will never cause you to take home less overall. Only the dollars above each threshold are taxed at the higher rate.

## Tips to Reduce Your Tax Bill

While you cannot change the bracket rates, there are several legitimate ways to lower the income that gets taxed:

- **Contribute to your RRSP.** Every dollar you contribute to a Registered Retirement Savings Plan reduces your taxable income by the same amount. The 2026 limit is $33,810, and contributions made by March 1, 2027 count for your 2026 return. Use our [RRSP calculator](/rrsp-calculator) to see how much you could save.
- **Maximize your TFSA.** While TFSA contributions are not tax-deductible, the investment growth inside the account is completely tax-free, which helps you keep more of your returns long-term.
- **Claim all eligible deductions.** Working from home, moving expenses for a new job, child care costs, and professional dues can all reduce your taxable income. Make sure you are not leaving money on the table.
- **Split pension income.** If you are 65 or older and receiving eligible pension income, you may be able to split up to 50% of that income with your spouse or common-law partner, which can lower both of your marginal rates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the basic personal amount for 2026?

The federal basic personal amount for 2026 is $16,452. This is the amount of income you can earn before any federal income tax applies (it shrinks to $14,829 at high incomes). Each province also has its own basic personal amount, which varies. For example, Alberta's is $22,769, while Ontario's is $12,989.

### Does earning more money ever result in less take-home pay?

No. Under Canada's progressive tax system, only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. Moving into a higher bracket does not retroactively increase the tax on income you already earned below that threshold. You will always take home more money (after tax) by earning more. There is no "cliff" where a raise costs you money.

### How is tax different for self-employed individuals?

If you are self-employed, the same federal and provincial tax brackets apply to your net business income (revenue minus eligible business expenses). The key difference is CPP contributions: employees split CPP with their employer, but self-employed individuals pay both halves. For 2026, that means a CPP rate of 11.9% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, plus 8% CPP2 on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000.
